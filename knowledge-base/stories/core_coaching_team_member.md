# Coaching a Team Member
## Solvex Pay - Senior Software Engineer, Money Movement Team

### Context
Rafael joined our team as a junior engineer straight out of a bootcamp, technically capable but visibly anxious in code reviews and design discussions — he'd go quiet in meetings and second-guess himself in PR comments even when his instincts were right. I wasn't his manager, but I ended up being his de facto mentor over his first six months on the team.

### Stakeholders
- Rafael (junior engineer being mentored)
- Engineering Manager (aware of and supportive of the informal mentoring)
- Money Movement Team (benefited from Rafael ramping up faster)

### Table of content
Informally mentored a junior teammate over six months, moving from pairing and explaining the "why" behind decisions, to deliberately stepping back and letting him drive, to publicly reinforcing his good calls so his confidence caught up with his skill.

### Actions
1. Started with regular pairing sessions on real tickets, narrating my reasoning out loud instead of just producing the answer, so he could see the "why," not just the "what."
2. Noticed he was often right in PR comments but phrased his suggestions as questions out of uncertainty — I started explicitly agreeing with and amplifying his good calls in front of the team.
3. Deliberately handed him increasingly ambiguous tickets and stepped back, resisting the urge to jump in early when he got stuck, and letting him ask for help rather than offering it preemptively.
4. Gave him direct, specific feedback after design discussions — what landed well, what to sharpen — instead of vague encouragement.
5. Checked in periodically outside of formal 1:1s, just to ask how he was finding the ramp-up, to keep the relationship informal and low-pressure.

### Result and impact
- Within six months, Rafael was leading small features independently and speaking up unprompted in design reviews.
- He later told our manager that the turning point was realizing his ideas were already good — he just needed to trust them.
- The pairing notes I kept became an informal onboarding guide that the team later used for the next two new hires.

### Learnings
- Confidence and competence often arrive on different timelines; sometimes the coaching job is closing that gap, not teaching new skills.
- Publicly reinforcing someone's good calls is one of the fastest ways to build their confidence — it carries more weight coming from a peer, in front of others, than in private.
- Stepping back deliberately, even when it's slower in the moment, is often the actual mentoring — jumping in to help too fast just extends the dependency.

### Signal areas
- **Growth**: Focused the mentoring on unlocking existing skill and confidence rather than just transferring knowledge.
- **Communication**: Gave direct, specific feedback and made a point of publicly reinforcing good decisions.
- **Leadership**: Took on an informal mentoring role without a formal title, and left behind a reusable onboarding resource for the team.
